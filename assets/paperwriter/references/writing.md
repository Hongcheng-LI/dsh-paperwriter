# Writing blueprint and drafting

## Guide the author, not just the document

Start from the author's present stage: understanding reference papers, finding a story in results, planning a section, evaluating a draft, or refining language. Give the next useful piece of writing help at that stage rather than presenting the entire workflow. If the author is unsure what to provide, request the most informative available material (for example a principal result, figure with its explanation, or existing paragraph), explain what it will help decide, and work from it.

Help the author identify the paper's central contribution in plain language: what was learned, what evidence makes it credible, and how it changes or extends the relevant prior knowledge. Offer a provisional formulation drawn from supplied material for the author to correct. Do not make the author solve abstract questions such as “what is your storyline?” without first helping interpret the results. When genuinely different narratives are possible, explain their tradeoffs and recommend the best-supported one; do not fabricate a dramatic gap to make a modest contribution sound larger.

Translate reference reading into writing instruction. Explain how a specific paper brings the reader from context to question, from observations to a finding, or from findings to contribution; then show what that organizational choice would mean for the author's material. The aim is transfer of a writing principle, not imitation of source wording or a lecture about journal conventions.

For an outline, make the narrative visible: give each proposed paragraph or subsection a purpose and explain how it advances the whole argument. Recommend what to emphasize and what to omit or relocate. For a draft, identify the most consequential writing issue, explain its effect on the reader, and demonstrate the improvement with revised text when requested. Avoid exhausting the author with equal-weight lists of every possible edit.

Use a natural exchange: editorial recommendation and reason, concrete outline or prose as appropriate, then the few author decisions or missing facts needed for the next step. This is a conversational pattern, not mandatory response headings. Ask targeted questions that help resolve a real writing choice, not repeated permission to proceed. Continue under existing authorization and accepted decisions.

In an established paragraph-by-paragraph writing collaboration, make useful analysis visible in a candidate passage during the same response; do not repeatedly stop at “here is how to organize it” and ask whether to rewrite. Lead with the consequential editorial judgment, provide the candidate in the manuscript language with the required paragraph rationale, and end with a small, prioritized set of unresolved items. Respect requests for diagnosis or discussion only: they do not authorize file edits, and any illustrative wording must be clearly a suggestion, not an applied revision.

A candidate can be useful before every fact is settled. Compose supported content into connected prose, using short issue-linked placeholders only where essential; keep assumptions, conditional alternatives and requests outside the passage. Do not turn an unverified control identity, cell-type mapping or conventional procedure into a fact to make the candidate read smoothly. Label its scope as a proposed working version rather than a final manuscript. A candidate shown in conversation does not replace the canonical file without authorization; paragraph locators may refer to that displayed version until it is adopted.

Build on feedback across the manuscript. If the author accepts a particular narrative emphasis, level of detail or expression preference, record it in project decisions and apply it to later sections where appropriate. Explain exceptions. Periodically check that the title, Introduction promise, Results emphasis and Conclusion contribution still tell the same supported story.

Success means the author receives stronger usable prose and understands the important editorial choices. Supporting records and verification protect that outcome; they are not the main user-facing product. Do not treat more instructions, more cautions or more artifacts as evidence of better writing.

## Blueprint and composition

First build manuscript scope from data, then compare it with the provisional journal architecture. State mismatches plainly. Discuss reducing claims, changing article type or additional evidence when needed; never manufacture significance to fit a journal.

The blueprint is instantiated from assets/templates/blueprint.md. Each proposed subsection answers a question with identified observations and sources, permitted interpretation, limitations and a figure/table/SI role. Order by reasoning dependencies, not filenames or experiment chronology. Preserve evidence that contradicts the preferred account.

Use one combined Gate 2 for evidence choices and the blueprint. Author acceptance already given remains valid. Ask only for decisions needed to settle scope, claim strength or analysis choices. Optional preference style should not create an extra gate.

Default writing order: Results with Methods, Discussion/Conclusions as applicable, Introduction, then Abstract/Title calibration. Adapt to article type and existing drafts. For each passage retrieve multi-source rhetorical patterns with paragraph context, consult the claim map and compose new language. Save external citations only where passages actually support the assertion.

Apply style through concrete choices (information order, transitions, detail, tense); avoid forced sentence-length limits, superlatives, fixed author mimicry or copying source wording with data substitutions.

## From argument to readable prose

Editorial selection belongs in the first draft, not only a later revision. Raw records determine facts but are not a manuscript outline. Before expanding them into prose, select the information needed for the section's purpose, assign supporting detail a destination, and choose a reader-friendly order. Do not deliver an exhaustive record transcription that requires the author to seek another editor for basic compression and prioritization.

Before drafting a section, state its intended takeaway and the evidence sequence briefly in the blueprint or working notes; do not create a new approval gate. Determine each paragraph's distinct job and its connection to the next. Then compose the paragraph from project facts and the learned organizational pattern, not by paraphrasing a source sentence. Evidence records support the writing; their field order should not dictate the prose.

- Introduction: select background examples for the inference they enable. Explain how prior knowledge leads to the present question or justified extension, rather than listing studies and appending “therefore”. Do not force an unanswered-question narrative onto a descriptive study, invent a scarcity claim, or call routine continuation necessary without support. The final paragraph should identify what this work actually investigates and contributes at the strength its evidence permits.
- Results and discussion: present the observations needed to reach a particular finding, followed by a proportionate interpretation. Use differential comparison for genuinely related cases after establishing the shared basis; retain the observations needed to support each difference and uncertainty. Avoid both a raw-data dump and conclusions stripped of their decisive evidence. The scientific question determines whether an integrated or separate Discussion fits.
- Activity or performance evaluation: state the tested scope and comparable quantitative results, with controls, conditions and uncertainty when available. Preserve relevant negative findings. Separate observed comparisons from tentative structure–activity relationships and mechanistic explanations; stronger language requires stronger evidence, not just an impressive comparator paper.
- Discussion (when separate): organize around the principal findings and what they mean in relation to prior work. Explain agreement, disagreement and alternative interpretations using relevant evidence; distinguish observed findings from proposed explanations. Integrate limitations where they change the interpretation, and derive implications or future work from specific unresolved questions rather than generic calls for more research.
- Title: name the actual subject and contribution at an appropriate level of specificity. Choose emphasis from the accepted story and journal examples; avoid vague importance claims, unsupported mechanisms and labels such as “novel” used instead of informative content. Refine the title when the main findings are settled.
- Abstract: follow the appropriate comparator's information priorities, select the central findings and necessary quantitative anchors, and avoid spending space on generic importance when the corpus is discovery-led. Do not enumerate every method or use methods absent from the project.
- Conclusion: synthesize the contribution and its supported implications instead of repeating the Abstract sentence by sentence. Match the scale of the closing claim to the study; generic praise is not a substitute for a specific contribution.

These are editorial questions, not a mandatory section template. Respect the actual article type, accepted outline and user's requested drafting order.

When drafting or revising Methods, read [methods.md](methods.md) for information selection, procedure organization and targeted author questions.

When evaluating edits to this skill's own drafts, read [revision-learning.md](revision-learning.md) to distinguish writing improvements from newly supplied evidence.

## Focused revision: editorial judgment before sentence polishing

When the author supplies a section for revision, first determine its role in the whole paper and the kind of help requested. A question about whether to include a figure calls for a recommendation with reasons, not automatic manuscript mutation. An explicit request to revise calls for usable revised prose as well as concise reasons; do not end by offering to do the already requested revision. Reuse accepted decisions and do not restart the manuscript workflow.

Allocate content by function, not by a blanket rule about section labels:
- Main text carries the contribution, decisive evidence and information needed to interpret it.
- Experimental carries reproducible procedures; retain conditions in Results when they define a comparison or constrain its interpretation.
- SI carries supporting detail and extended records that remain accessible and cited. A screening figure may belong in SI when it supports selection, but in the main text when the screening comparison itself is a central finding. Do not prescribe figure placement from journal identity alone.
- Revision notes carry editorial tasks, missing-file requests and verification questions. Scientific uncertainty is not merely an editorial task: preserve consequential qualifiers in the manuscript and do not remove them just to make a paragraph sound finished.

Consider merging a short subsection when it adds no distinct question or result. For example, routine identification of known entities may need only a concise statement with the comparison basis and references; new or ambiguous assignments still need their evidence. Recommend the destination of displaced information rather than silently deleting it. If authorized changes move a figure or merge a section, check dependent numbering and cross-references against the actual files; a proposed numbering scheme is not a verified current scheme.

Make caution proportionate and local. Express the tested range, endpoint and tentative status in the relevant claim; put extended audit discussion in review notes unless it materially changes interpretation. Do not replace a specific limitation with a vague disclaimer while retaining a stronger claim. In particular, uncertain reproducibility does not disappear because a sentence says “suggested”, and “associated with” does not by itself establish a reliable relationship.

Run a factual-delta check between the supplied and revised text. Besides numbers and identifiers, inspect added methods, selection reasons, chronology, comparative adjectives, causal links and completion status. Smooth transitions can invent research history: “considering X, Y and Z, condition A was prioritized” requires evidence of that decision, not merely the existence of measurements X, Y and Z. A planned analysis must not become a completed past-tense method. Keep conditional proposed text separate until its condition is verified.

For a learned rewrite to count as useful, it must improve focus, evidence order or readability without introducing unsupported facts or concealing unresolved science. Judge the actual revised passage against these criteria, not whether it resembles an admired response. State separately whether the result is a language revision, an evidence-checked draft or an author-approved final version.

## Paragraph-level editorial rationale

For initial drafting and substantial restructuring of any manuscript section, present each paragraph followed by a short editorial explanation. Use the agreed manuscript language for the paragraph and the user's collaboration language for the explanation (English text plus Chinese explanation by default for Chinese-speaking users). Provide observable editorial reasons and evidence references, not a transcript of internal deliberation.

Cover four points, usually one sentence each:
- Purpose: what the reader should understand after this paragraph.
- Content and order: why these details belong here and how the paragraph connects to its neighbors.
- Basis: identify relevant project evidence/claim IDs, background references, and corpus source or pattern IDs. Distinguish factual support from stylistic guidance; mark unavailable support rather than inventing a citation or claiming a journal convention without evidence.
- Boundaries: explain consequential qualifiers and conclusions the available evidence does not support. If no special limitation applies, say so briefly instead of inventing one.

Save the paired review version in 05_drafts/revision_notes/<section_slug>_paragraph_review.md, using a paragraph identifier or section-and-paragraph locator and the canonical manuscript version/date. Generate its manuscript excerpts from the canonical text; it is a review artifact, not a second editable master. In an existing project with an established revision directory, use that directory and record its location instead of reorganizing the project.

Keep explanations out of the canonical manuscript, clean section drafts and submission exports. No separate approval is required for each paragraph; retain the existing section/blueprint review workflow. For minor language edits, update only affected explanations when their rationale changes. If paragraphs are split, merged or moved, update their locators and neighboring transitions. Before delivering a reviewed section, check that every newly drafted or substantially restructured paragraph has an explanation and that excerpts match the identified manuscript version.

Canonical manuscript: agree one authoritative editable file and record its path in project.json. If user edits an exported Word version, inspect changes and reconcile before further regeneration. Never overwrite the author's edits by re-exporting an older Markdown file. Section previews are derived views, not independent masters.

Revision passes:
1. Scientific: data, methods, controls, scope, claims and citation support.
2. Structural: reasoning order, repetition, figures, limitations and SI.
3. Language: terminology, journal patterns, readability and optional preferences.
Log material changes with source and affected claim IDs. Never strengthen a claim in the language pass.

Use actual available tools for charts and document exports; prefer editable source figures. Render and inspect final layouts. A graphical abstract or cover letter is generated only when required/requested and supported by the project.
